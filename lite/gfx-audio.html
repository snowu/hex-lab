Audio as a system

Playing a sound is easy; making a mix that works, adapts and does not fatigue is the craft.

Why audio gets neglected, and why it should not

Sound carries a large part of game feel (the juice lesson's simulation makes it obvious), gives information the screen cannot (something behind you, a low-health warning), and sets emotion. It is also easy to get wrong in ways players feel but cannot name: harsh repetition, clipping, music that fights sound effects, and sounds that arrive late.

The basics

  • Sample rate and bit depth: 44.1 or 48 kHz, 16 bit is the working standard. Higher helps only during editing.
  • Formats: WAV (uncompressed): fastest to play, large, good for short effects. OGG Vorbis or MP3 (compressed): small, for music and long ambience, and costs a little CPU to decode. Choose per sound: short and frequent → uncompressed in memory; long → compressed and streamed.
  • Mono vs stereo: positional (3D or panned) sounds should be mono; the engine does the panning. Stereo is for music and non-positional ambience.
  • Loudness: normalise your assets to a consistent perceived level (targets vary by platform: roughly −16 LUFS for mobile and −24 to −20 for living-room consoles are commonly cited, so check the platform's guidance). Nothing should clip: if the sum of voices exceeds full scale the output distorts, so leave headroom and use a limiter on the master bus.

Buses and mixing

Route sounds into buses: Music, SFX, Voice, UI, Ambience, each with its own volume, effects and mute, all feeding a Master. Reasons: player volume sliders per category (an accessibility need), ducking (automatically lower music while dialogue plays), reverb sends for caves, a low-pass filter on everything except the pause menu when the game is paused, and sensible limiting. Set the structure up early; retrofitting buses is painful.

Variation: the cure for fatigue

A footstep that plays the identical sample 500 times becomes a machine gun. Fixes, cheapest first: random pitch within a few percent, random volume, round-robin or random choice among several variants (never the same one twice in a row), and slightly different timing. A good rule: anything heard more than a handful of times a minute needs variation.

Positional audio

  • Attenuation: volume falls off with distance (inverse-distance or a curve you control) to a maximum range.
  • Panning: left/right based on direction relative to the listener. 3D audio adds height and behind-ness cues via HRTF on headphones.
  • Doppler and occlusion (muffle a sound behind a wall with a low-pass filter) are optional polish.
  • For 2D games use a listener at the camera or player and tune the attenuation range to the screen size: sounds just off screen should be quiet but audible, not silent.

Managing many voices

A busy scene can request hundreds of sounds a frame. Hardware and mixing time are limited, so engines cap the number of simultaneous voices. Policies: drop the quietest or the oldest, limit per sound (at most 3 footsteps at once), and prioritise (player's gun above distant enemy gunfire). This is the event queue problem from the patterns track, with the "full queue" policy being a design decision.

Music that adapts

  • Loops: seamless loop points need a sample-accurate loop and a tail that matches the start; compressed formats can add small gaps, so verify.
  • Layers (stems): record music as separate parts (drums, bass, melody) and fade layers in and out with game intensity. Cheap and powerful.
  • Transitions: switch tracks on the beat or bar with a short crossfade instead of cutting, using tempo information to schedule the change.
  • Silence is part of the design: constant music wears the player out. Space it out, let ambience carry exploration.

Technical traps

  • Latency: buffer sizes trade glitch-resistance for delay. A shot that sounds 150 ms late feels wrong. Web and mobile have more latency than desktop; keep the buffers as small as stable.
  • Browser autoplay rule: browsers block audio until the user interacts. Start the audio context after the first click or tap (the juice simulation in this site does exactly this).
  • Pausing: decide which buses pause with the game and which keep playing (menu sounds).
  • Threading: audio runs on its own thread; do not do slow work in its callback and do not share unsynchronised state with the game thread.
  • Memory: decompressed music in RAM is large. Stream long files.

Making sound

Free and cheap tools: sfxr/jsfxr and Bfxr for retro effects; Audacity for editing; LMMS or Reaper for music; field recordings and CC0 libraries (freesound.org, Kenney) for raw material. Layering two or three simple sounds (a thump, a crack, a tail) beats one perfect sample.
