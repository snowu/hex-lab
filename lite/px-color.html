Colour: palettes, ramps and hue shifting

Why limited palettes work, how to build a ramp, and the one trick that makes flat art look lit.

Limited palettes

A small palette forces cohesion: every asset in a game shares the same colours, so things look like they belong together. Typical sizes are 8 to 32 colours for a game (16 for a single sprite is already plenty). Pre-made palettes are on Lospec (the palette list there is the standard reference). Well-known ones include PICO-8 (16), Sweetie 16, Endesga 32 and 64, Resurrect 64 and Apollo. Pick one early and stick with it; changing the palette later means re-colouring everything (indexed colour and palette swaps help, see the workflow lesson).

Value first

Colour has hue (which colour), saturation (how vivid) and value (how light or dark). The eye reads value before hue. Squint or switch the preview to greyscale: if the sprite does not separate into light, mid and dark in greyscale, colour will not fix it. Build contrast with value; use hue for mood and grouping.

Ramps

A ramp is a short run of colours from dark to light for one material (3 to 5 steps is usually enough: shadow, base, light, maybe highlight). Draw each material from one ramp, share ramps between objects of the same material, and you stay inside the palette budget.

Hue shifting

A ramp made by only changing lightness looks muddy and flat: it is the colour of the object under a grey light. Real light has colour. A common, effective convention is: highlights drift toward the light colour (warmer, towards yellow) and shadows drift toward the ambient/sky colour (cooler, towards blue or purple), along with small changes in saturation (shadows and lights often a little less saturated than mid-tones, extremes avoided). Adjust it by scene: a night scene or a lava level reverses the warm and cool roles.

  • Shift by about 10 to 30 degrees per step, not 90.
  • Avoid pure black and pure white for shading. A very dark purple or navy is a better “black”; an off-white with a hint of yellow is a better “white”. Save the extremes for outlines or sparkles if at all.
  • Keep saturation under control: a saturated sprite on a saturated background is unreadable. Reserve the most vivid colours for the things the player must notice.
  • Build colour hierarchy: the focal object gets the highest contrast and saturation, the background the least.

Simulation: Build a ramp, shift the hue

Both balls use the same number of colours and the same light direction. The left ramp changes only lightness. The right one also shifts the hue between shadow and highlight.

Try this in the full app:

  • Slide hue shift from 0 up to ~30° and compare the two spheres: the right one starts to look lit by a warm light.
  • Change the base hue (try blue, red, green) and see that the effect works for any colour.
  • Reduce steps to 3: a tiny ramp can still be enough for a small sprite.
  • Raise the shift to 60: it starts to look like separate colours, not one material. Too much is as wrong as none.

Answer key

1. Quick check back ↑

B. View it in greyscale (or squint): the light/mid/dark masses should still read

Value carries the form. If greyscale looks flat, colour changes will only disguise the problem.

2. Quick check back ↑

B. Shadows lean blue/teal, base is green, highlights lean yellow-green

Highlights pick up the warm light, shadows pick up the cool ambient light. This hue shift is what makes it feel lit.

3. Quick check back ↑

B. It keeps the palette small and the scene visually consistent

Cohesion is the main benefit: the grass, bush and tree leaves all belong to the same world because they share colours.

4. Check 1 back ↑

D. Use a very dark purple or navy instead of black and an off-white with a warm tint instead of white

Extremes should be saved for outlines or sparkles; near-blacks and off-whites shade far more naturally.

5. Check 2 back ↑

A. Reserve the highest contrast and saturation for the focal object and mute the background

The eye goes to contrast and vividness, so backgrounds stay quieter while the thing the player must notice stays loudest.
