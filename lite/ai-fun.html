AI that is fun: fairness, directors and testing

Believable beats smart: telegraphing, attack tokens, directors and dynamic difficulty, cheating, and how to test AI.

The goal is fun, not intelligence

A perfect enemy that headshots you instantly is not good AI, it is a bad game. Players enjoy enemies that are readable (I can see what it is about to do), fair (I could have avoided it) and expressive (it reacts to what I did). Design for those three, and let the technique follow.

Telegraph before acting

A wind-up animation, a sound, a laser sight, a spoken line, a short pause before the strike: these give the player time to react and are what makes a fight feel like a conversation instead of dice. The cheaper the attack to dodge, the shorter the telegraph can be; the stronger the attack, the longer it must be. Likewise, tell the player what the AI knows (the "!" above the guard's head).

Make small mistakes on purpose

  • Reaction time: delay responses by 150 to 400 ms.
  • Imperfect aim: bullets miss the first shots, tighten when the player stands still, and never hit the player on the opening shot of an encounter if that feels unfair (missing deliberately but visibly "near" is a classic trick).
  • Hesitation and variety: do not always pick the optimal action. Random choice weighted by score avoids the "always the same" feeling.

Group AI: attack tokens

Five enemies all rushing the player at once feels cheap and unreadable. A common fix is a token system: the group has a small number of "attack tokens" (say 2). An enemy must hold a token to attack; the rest circle, taunt or flank. When an attack ends the token returns. The player always faces a manageable number of threats, and the encounter looks coordinated. Related ideas: assign roles (a tank, a flanker, a ranged support), keep a minimum spacing, and use barks (spoken lines) so the player hears the AI's plan.

Directors and pacing

An AI director watches the player's state (health, recent stress, time since the last fight) and controls what happens rather than any single agent: when to spawn more enemies, what items to drop, when to give a quiet moment. Left 4 Dead popularised it: intensity builds, peaks, then relaxes, so the pacing varies by itself and each run differs. A minimal director is a number (intensity) that rises with combat and falls with calm, and a table of "if intensity is low and time since a fight is high, spawn a wave".

Dynamic difficulty and rubber banding

  • Quiet adjustment (slightly fewer enemies after several deaths, more health drops) can help struggling players. Be careful: when players notice a game cheating against them (racing rubber banding), trust is lost, and when it helps them too obviously, wins feel hollow.
  • Prefer visible choices (difficulty levels, assist options) and keep adjustments modest, symmetric and explainable.
  • Always allow the player to turn adaptation off.

Cheating AI, honestly

Many shipped AIs cheat: strategy AIs ignore fog of war or gain resources, racing AIs get speed boosts, shooters spawn enemies outside the camera view. It is often the right trade (cheaper than simulating everything). Rules: cheat in the player's favour of fun, never in a way the player would catch and resent, and keep the cheat invisible when it removes challenge and visible when it adds fairness.

Testing and debugging AI

  • Visualise: draw state names, current target, perception cones, paths, scores, token holders above or around each agent, and show the active branch of the tree. Make it a toggle.
  • Reproduce: a fixed random seed and a recorded input stream turn "the guard got stuck once" into a repeatable test.
  • Bots: automated players that run thousands of encounters headless, measure win rates, time to kill and deaths per room, and catch broken balance overnight (the same idea as the balance benchmarks in your own game).
  • Scenario tests: small hand-built rooms ("guard, wall, player behind it") with assertions: the guard must find the player within N seconds.
  • Metrics from playtests: where players die, how long fights last, how often the AI loses track.

A checklist for each enemy type

  1. What does the player see before it attacks (telegraph)?
  2. How can the player beat it (the counter)? Is the counter learnable?
  3. What does it do when it loses the player?
  4. What does it do with two or ten of its friends around?
  5. What is its worst failure mode (stuck, jitter, suicide off a ledge) and how would you detect it?

Design exercise: a boss that teaches

Design a boss with three attacks. For each one write: the telegraph (what the player sees or hears, and how long), the counter (what the player does), and the punish window (when the player may attack safely).

  • How does the boss change when the player is at low health? (Longer telegraphs, never a new unreadable attack.)
  • What does the director do if the player dies five times in a row? (Offer a health pickup, or a longer punish window, or a visible assist option.)
  • Which information must stay on screen at all times?
