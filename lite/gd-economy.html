Progression, economies and randomness

Sinks and faucets, exponential cost curves, why random rewards work, and pity timers.

Progression types

  • Power progression: you get stronger (levels, gear). Risks trivialising content, so the world must scale with you.
  • Knowledge/skill progression: you get better (Dark Souls, roguelikes). Power barely changes, the player does.
  • Content progression: new places, abilities that unlock routes (metroidvanias).
  • Meta progression: persistent upgrades between runs (Hades mirror, roguelite unlocks).

Economies: sources and sinks

Any currency has faucets (ways to earn) and sinks (ways to spend). If faucets outpace sinks the currency inflates and loses meaning; if sinks dominate the game feels like a grind. Track flows in a spreadsheet before building: income per minute, price of the next upgrade, and the time between purchases. You want the player to make a meaningful choice every few minutes, not wait.

Cost curves

Costs usually grow exponentially (cost = base × growth^level), while income grows linearly or slower. That keeps the time between upgrades growing and eventually forces a reset or a new system (prestige). A growth factor of roughly 1.07 to 1.15 per level is typical for idle games. You implement the curve in the drill.

Randomness

  • Input randomness (what you get dealt, loot, level layout) creates variety and stories. Output randomness (does my attack hit?) can feel unfair.
  • Humans are poor at probability: they expect streaks to end. Pure independent rolls produce long droughts that feel broken. Designers use pseudo-random distribution (the chance rises after each failure), bad-luck protection and shuffle bags (draw without replacement, so every item shows up regularly).
  • Pity timers guarantee a reward after N misses, bounding the worst case. You implement one below.
  • Expected value: an event with chance p takes 1/p tries on average, but the median is shorter and the tail is long (with p = 1%, 37% of players have no drop after 100 tries).
Ethics and law: variable rewards plus spending is the loot-box problem. Several countries regulate or ban paid random boxes and platforms require disclosed odds. Design retention around skill and content, not around exploiting the way brains react to random reward.

Drill: Drop rates with a pity timer

Loot systems use a pity timer so bad luck is capped. rolls are random floats in [0, 1). A roll drops the item if roll < p. Additionally, after pity - 1 misses in a row the next roll is a guaranteed drop. Any drop resets the miss counter. Write out one boolean per roll.

Starter (Zig):

const std = @import("std");

/// out[i] = whether roll i dropped the item. out.len == rolls.len.
pub fn simulatePity(rolls: []const f32, p: f32, pity: u32, out: []bool) void {
    _ = rolls;
    _ = p;
    _ = pity;
    for (out) |*o| o.* = false;
}

Answer key

1. Drop rates with a pity timer back ↑

Hint: Keep misses = 0. For each roll: hit = roll < p or misses + 1 >= pity. If hit, misses = 0, else misses += 1.

With a flat 1% rate, 37% of players still have not seen the item after 100 rolls (0.99^100). A pity timer changes the feel from "unlucky" to "bounded". Many games add soft pity: the rate ramps up after N misses. Disclose the odds: it is increasingly a legal requirement too.

2. Exponential upgrade costs back ↑

Hint: Loop: spent = 0, level = 0; while spent + cost(level) <= budget { spent += cost(level); level += 1 }. pow(growth, level) returns a float, so floor it before converting to an integer.

Cost grows exponentially while income usually grows only linearly or polynomially, so the time between upgrades keeps stretching. That tension is the whole design of an incremental game: a growth factor around 1.07 to 1.15 per level is common (Cookie Clicker buildings use 1.15). Resets ("prestige") trade progress for a multiplier and restart the curve.

3. Quick check back ↑

A. Pacing problem: faucets outrun sinks (inflation)

Add meaningful sinks (cosmetics, upgrades with steep curves, repair costs) or slow the faucets. Check every currency flow.

4. Quick check back ↑

B. 20 on average, but a meaningful fraction of players need 40 or more (bad luck is common), so add pity

Mean = 1/p = 20. With independent rolls 0.95^40 is about 13% still without it. Pity timers or a shuffle bag bound the bad cases.

5. Check 1 back ↑

A. So the time between upgrades keeps growing and forces meaningful choices or a reset

Exponential costs against slower income growth stretch the wait between purchases, which is what keeps choices meaningful.

6. Check 2 back ↑

B. Input randomness (what you are dealt) creates variety; output randomness (whether your action works) feels unfair

Variety before the decision delights; randomness that vetoes the decision after it frustrates.
