Animation

Frame counts, timing, the animation principles that matter at small sizes, and walk cycles.

Fewer frames, better timing

Pixel art animation is usually low frame count (often 6 to 12 frames per second of animation, not 60). The skill is choosing which poses to draw and how long to hold each. Holding a pose for two or three ticks gives weight; every frame at the same duration looks robotic. Most tools (and Godot's AnimatedSprite2D or AnimationPlayer) let you set per-frame durations, so you do not need extra drawings to slow a part down.

Principles that pay off at 16×16

  • Squash and stretch: compress on impact and stretch at speed. Keep the volume believable (wider means shorter). A bouncing ball is the classic exercise (use the simulation).
  • Anticipation: a small opposite motion before the action (crouch before jump, wind-up before a swing). It tells the player the attack is coming, which is also a gameplay readability tool.
  • Follow-through and overlap: hair, scarf and tails lag the body and settle after it stops.
  • Ease in/out (spacing): put the drawings close together at the start and end of a motion and further apart in the middle.
  • Arcs: limbs and bodies move along curves, not straight lines.
  • Key frames first: draw the extreme poses, then the in-betweens.

Common cycles

  • Idle: 2 to 4 frames, a slow breathing bob of a pixel or two. Sync with a subtle highlight.
  • Walk: four key poses (contact, down, passing, up) mirrored for the other leg gives an 8-frame cycle. A 4-frame walk reads fine for small sprites. Bob the body one pixel on the “down” frames.
  • Run: same with more lean and a longer stride and airborne frames.
  • Attack: anticipation (long hold), strike (very short, often one frame with a smear), recovery. Hold the contact frame for a tick to sell impact (the hit stop idea from the juice lesson).
  • Hurt and death: one flash frame (white) plus a knockback pose.

Practicalities

  • Consistent canvas and origin: every frame on the same canvas size, with the character's feet (the pivot) at the same pixel, or it will jitter in game.
  • Use onion skinning (ghost the previous frame) to check spacing.
  • Export a sprite sheet (a grid of frames in one PNG) plus frame size and durations. Aseprite can export a sheet and a JSON description.
  • Test in the engine with the real camera and speed. An animation that looks good looping in the editor can fight the movement speed (“moonwalking”). Match stride to velocity.

All frames must share a canvas and an anchor (usually the feet) or the drawing shifts when frames change.

Volume preservation is what sells squash and stretch; stretching one way must compress the other.
