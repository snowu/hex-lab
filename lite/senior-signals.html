What “senior” means in an interview

Scope, ambiguity, trade-offs, ownership, multiplying others.

Mid-level engineers are evaluated on whether they can solve the problem. Senior engineers are evaluated on whether they pick the right problem, solve it in a way the team can live with, and leave others better off. Interviewers listen for five signals:

SignalWhat it sounds like
Scope“Before I design anything, who are the users and what happens if this is down for an hour?”
AmbiguityYou state assumptions out loud, pick defaults, and move on instead of waiting to be told.
Trade-offsEvery decision has a “because” and a “this costs us…”. No silver bullets.
OwnershipYou talk about operating it: on-call, rollouts, metrics, incidents, cost.
MultiplyingMentoring, writing design docs, unblocking, raising the bar in review.
Rule of thumb: if you answer in the form “Option A is X, option B is Y, I would choose A because Z, and I would revisit if W”, you already sound senior.

Self-check: your five stories

Prepare five real stories you can bend to many questions: (1) a technical decision with trade-offs, (2) a production incident you owned, (3) a conflict or disagreement, (4) a time you raised the bar for others, (5) a failure. Write each as: context → your decision → result with a number → what you would do differently.

Answer key

1. Quick check back ↑

B. Ask clarifying questions, then state assumptions and a rough scale

Clarifying and then committing to explicit assumptions shows you can drive under ambiguity. Handing the problem back (option 3) is the classic mid-level tell.

2. Quick check back ↑

B. “We chose a queue here; it adds operational cost and makes debugging harder, but it protects the bank API from our burst traffic.”

Naming the cost of your choice, and what it buys you, is the core senior skill.

3. Check 1 back ↑

B. State your assumptions out loud, pick sensible defaults, and move on

The ambiguity signal is stating assumptions and committing to defaults instead of waiting to be told.

4. Check 2 back ↑

B. The trade-off: what the choice costs and what it buys, plus when you would revisit it

Senior answers name the cost of the choice and the revisit condition. “Industry standard” is not a reason.
