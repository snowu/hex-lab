A framework you can run under pressure

Requirements → estimates → API → data → architecture → deep dives → failure.

Interviewers do not want your memorised architecture. They want to watch you structure an open problem. Step through the framework below, then see it applied end to end in the webhook worked design and practise it on the Design lab reps.

1. Clarify & scope (3-5 min)

Functional requirements (the 3-4 things the system must do) and what is explicitly out of scope. Ask who the users are and what “correct” means. Money? Then correctness beats latency.

2. Non-functional & numbers

Availability target, latency target (p50/p99), consistency needs, durability, data retention, regulatory limits. Back-of-envelope: requests/sec = users × actions/day ÷ 86,400, peak ≈ 3-10× average. Storage = items × size × retention.

10M users × 5 actions/day = 50M/day ≈ 580 rps average, ~3k rps peak

3. API & data model

Sketch 3-5 endpoints (with idempotency on writes), and the core entities with their keys. State your read/write ratio: it drives storage and caching choices.

4. High-level design

Client → edge/LB → stateless services → data stores, plus async paths (queues, workers). Keep boxes few and each one justified by a requirement.

5. Deep dives

Pick the 1-2 hardest parts (the interviewer will nudge you). Typical: data partitioning, hot keys, exactly-once effects, fan-out, ranking, consistency between stores.

6. Failure, scale, operate

What breaks first? Retries, timeouts, backpressure, degradation modes, observability (RED/USE metrics, traces), rollout/rollback, cost. This step is what separates senior from mid.

Running the clock (45-minute round)

MinutesPhaseYou are done when…
0-5Clarify & scope3-4 functional requirements written down, out-of-scope list said out loud

38-45Operate & wrap upwhat breaks first, how you would know, what you would build next

The deep dive is where the hire/no-hire signal lives, so protect those 18 minutes. Candidates who spend 25 minutes on requirements and boxes never get there.

Reading the interviewer

  • A question is usually a steer, not a challenge. “What happens if this queue backs up?” means “go deeper here”. Follow it, then come back to your plan.
  • “Is that the only option?” means name an alternative and the trade-off. It rarely means you are wrong.
  • Silence after an answer means they want the next level: numbers, a failure case, or how you would operate it.
  • Ask to choose when time is short: “I can go deep on partitioning or on exactly-once delivery. Which is more useful to you?” Asking is a senior move; waiting is not.

When you are behind

Say so and cut explicitly: “I will skip detailed API shapes and come back if we have time.” A named cut reads as prioritisation; a silent one reads as a gap.

How design rounds are usually lost

  • Gold-plating: Kafka, Kubernetes and three caches for 50 requests per second. Every box must answer a requirement or a number.
  • No numbers: “It needs to scale” without rps or storage makes every later choice unjustified.
  • Breadth with no depth: twelve boxes, none explained to the level of keys, indexes or failure behaviour.
  • The happy path only: no timeouts, retries, duplicates or what the user sees when a dependency is down.
  • Defending instead of updating: when the interviewer adds a constraint, change the design and say why. Changing your mind with a reason is a strength.
Practise it timed: the Design lab has 10 reps that walk these six steps with a clock, hints and a reference to compare against. Start with the warm-up (URL shortener), then the rate limiter and webhooks, then the hard ones (ledger, pay by bank, offline sync).
