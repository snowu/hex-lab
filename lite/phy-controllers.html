Character controllers and platformer physics

Why most player characters are not rigid bodies, how move-and-slide works, and the bugs everyone hits with slopes and platforms.

Kinematic, not dynamic

You could make the player a rigid body pushed by forces. It feels terrible: the character slides on ice after releasing the stick, bounces off walls, gets launched by small collisions, and nothing is precisely tunable. Games with tight controls use a kinematic character controller: your code decides the velocity directly (accelerate to a target speed, jump with a fixed impulse), and the physics engine's job is only to move the body as far as it can without penetrating anything. Godot's CharacterBody2D with move_and_slide(), Unity's CharacterController and Unreal's CharacterMovementComponent are all this.

Move and slide, step by step

  1. Start with the desired motion this frame: motion = velocity * dt.
  2. Sweep the character's shape along motion (a shape cast). If nothing is hit, move the whole way and stop.
  3. If something is hit at fraction t: move to the contact point, keep the remaining motion (1 - t) * motion, and remove the component that points into the surface using the contact normal: remaining -= normal * dot(remaining, normal) (this is the "slide along the wall" projection from the vectors lesson).
  4. Repeat from step 2 with the slid remainder, up to a small number of times (typically 4). The limit stops infinite loops in tight corners.
  5. Record what you touched: floor, wall, ceiling, based on the normal's angle. Zero the velocity component into floors and ceilings so gravity does not accumulate while standing.

The things that make it feel good (and bug)

  • Floor angle: a surface is "floor" if its normal is within a maximum slope angle (say 45°) of up. Steeper surfaces are walls.
  • Snap to floor: when running down a slope or a step, the character would briefly leave the ground each frame. After moving, if the character was on the floor and is now just above one, push it down a few pixels so it stays grounded (and so jumping and animations work).
  • Slope speed: moving along the floor surface at a constant speed (project the horizontal velocity onto the slope direction) keeps speed constant uphill; otherwise uphill is slow and downhill hops.
  • Step up: automatically climb small ledges (stairs) by testing "if I lift by the step height, is the way forward free?".
  • One-way platforms: solid only when moving down onto them from above (check that the character was above the platform's top last frame, and that its velocity is downward). Let the player drop through by ignoring the platform for a short time.
  • Moving platforms: carry the character by the platform's frame movement while grounded on it (add the platform velocity to the character's motion), including rotating platforms.
  • Ceiling bonk and corner correction: if the head hits only the corner of a ceiling, nudge sideways instead of stopping the jump.
  • Coyote time, jump buffering, variable jump height: the input-forgiveness tricks of the first track layer on top of "am I grounded?".

Skin width

Floating-point error means "exactly touching" is unstable: the shape alternates between touching and slightly penetrating. Controllers keep a tiny skin gap (0.01 to 0.1 units) between the character and surfaces so contacts are always clean. If your character "vibrates" against a wall, that is the margin being too small.

Platformer feel is tuned, not simulated

Celeste, Super Mario and Hollow Knight use hand-designed rules: different gravity going up and going down (heavier fall), a jump that is cut short when the button is released, horizontal acceleration that is faster in the air to turn, max fall speed. Express them as constants you can tune in the inspector, and describe them with time and distance ("jump apex in 0.4 s, 4 tiles high") rather than raw gravity numbers: gravity = 2 * height / time² and jump_velocity = 2 * height / time.

Answer key

1. Spot the character controller bugs back ↑

Hint: One is about gravity and time, two are about "fixing" wall contact by hand.

  • Line 2: velocity.y += gravity is per frame (not multiplied by delta) and is added even while standing on the floor, so downward speed keeps building. Use gravity * delta and zero it (or only apply it) when not on the floor.
  • Line 10: Manually moving the position out of the wall bypasses the collision system and can push the body into other geometry or cause jitter. move_and_slide() already slides along walls.
  • Line 9: Zeroing horizontal velocity on any wall contact makes the player stick when holding toward the wall while falling or jumping, and kills momentum. Let sliding resolve it; the next frame's input recomputes velocity.x anyway.
