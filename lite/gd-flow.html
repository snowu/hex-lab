Flow, difficulty and teaching without text

Keeping players between bored and overwhelmed, and the level-design pattern behind the best tutorials.

The flow channel

Csikszentmihalyi's flow is the state of being absorbed when challenge roughly matches skill. Too easy and the player is bored, too hard and they are anxious. As skill grows, the challenge has to grow with it, or the player falls out of the channel. Level and difficulty design is mostly about moving the player along that channel, with rest beats between peaks so they can recover.

Difficulty curves

  • Not a straight ramp: a sawtooth (tension, release, a bit higher tension) feels better than a monotone climb.
  • Difficulty has several independent axes: execution (reflexes), knowledge (what to do), decision complexity and resource pressure. Raise one at a time.
  • Dynamic difficulty (quietly adjusting) can help, but players hate being rubber-banded when they notice. Offering a visible difficulty choice, or assist options, respects them.
  • Fail states: make failing fast to recover from. Short respawn and a checkpoint just before the hard part beat a punishing penalty. Celeste lets you retry in a fraction of a second.

Teach by doing

Players skip text. Good games teach through the level itself, commonly with a four-beat structure (often called Nintendo's level design approach, from the Japanese ki-shō-ten-ketsu):

  1. Introduce a mechanic in a safe place where failing costs nothing.
  2. Develop it: use it with small variations and mild risk.
  3. Twist: combine it with something unexpected or flip the context.
  4. Conclude: a final test that pays off the idea, then move on without ever repeating it.

The opening of Super Mario Bros. level 1-1 is the famous example: a lone goomba you cannot avoid teaches stomping in a safe space; a block row teaches jumping into blocks and mushrooms; a pit-free start lets you experiment before it adds gaps.

Readability and feedback

  • Telegraph before you punish: the enemy winds up, the floor glows, a sound plays.
  • Be consistent: if red means danger, never use red for a safe thing.
  • The player should always know why they died. “Unfair” almost always means “I did not understand what happened.”

Answer key

1. Quick check back ↑

B. Telegraph attacks, add a checkpoint right before the boss, and shorten the retry time

Legibility (telegraphing) plus fast recovery fixes the cause of frustration. Pure HP tuning may make the boss trivial for others without making failure understandable.

2. Quick check back ↑

B. Show it in a safe spot where the player can observe it and fail cheaply

The first beat is safe exposure. Complexity and risk come in the following beats.

A. A sawtooth with rest beats: tension, release, then slightly higher tension

Challenge must grow with skill along the flow channel, with recovery beats between peaks instead of a monotone climb.

B. Telegraph attacks before they land, keep danger cues consistent, and make retries fast with a nearby checkpoint

Unfair almost always means unread: telegraph before punishing and make failure fast to understand and recover from.
