Fundamentals: resolution, lines and shapes

What makes pixel art pixel art, how big to draw, and how to get clean lines. Draw in the built-in editor.

Every pixel is a decision

Pixel art is drawing where you control each pixel on a coarse grid. The constraint is the point: with only a handful of pixels you must decide what matters (shape, silhouette, one highlight) and drop the rest. Good pixel art is readable at actual size. If you need to zoom to understand it, simplify.

Resolution: pick it once

  • A game has a base resolution (for example 320×180, 384×216 or 480×270) that is scaled up by a whole number. 320×180 scales exactly to 1280×720 (×4) and 1920×1080 (×6).
  • Sprite sizes are chosen from it: 8×8 icons and tiny tiles, 16×16 characters and tiles (very common), 32×32 detailed characters, 64×64 and up for bosses.
  • Smaller canvases are faster to draw and animate and force stylisation. Larger ones show more detail but cost more per animation frame.
  • Never mix pixel sizes in one scene (“mixels”): a scaled-up 16×16 sprite next to a native 32×32 one looks wrong because the pixel grid sizes differ. Never rotate or scale pixel art by arbitrary angles in the engine; redraw or use a dedicated rotation algorithm (rotsprite).

Clean lines

  • Aim for lines made of even runs: for a shallow slope use 3-3-3 or 2-2-2, not 3-1-4-2. Runs that step smoothly look intentional.
  • Remove doubles: an L-shaped corner (a pixel touching its neighbours on two sides) makes the line look thick and lumpy. This is the “pixel perfect” cleanup that many editors have as a toggle.
  • Jaggies are the stair-steps that break a curve. A curve should shorten its runs gradually (6-3-2-1-1-2-3-6), never zig-zag.
  • Start with a silhouette in one flat colour. If it reads as the thing (a slime, a sword, a hero), add colour and detail. If not, no detail will fix it.
  • Use clusters: group same-colour pixels into deliberate shapes. Scattered single pixels look like noise.
  • Avoid pillow shading: shading that follows the outline (light in the centre, dark all around) ignores the light source and flattens the form. Pick a light direction (the next lessons).

Tools

  • Aseprite (paid, or compile it yourself from source), LibreSprite (free fork), Pixelorama (free, open source, made in Godot), Piskel (free, browser). A mouse is perfectly fine.
  • Learn four tools: pencil, eraser, fill, colour pick, plus a mirror/symmetry mode and a layer for sketching. The editor below has them.
  • Work zoomed in to draw and zoomed out (1× and 2×) to judge. The preview next to the canvas shows real size.

Answer key

1. Quick check back ↑

A. 6 (an integer)

1920 / 320 = 6 and 1080 / 180 = 6. Non-integer scaling makes some pixels 1 screen pixel wider than others, which shimmers when things move.

2. Quick check back ↑

B. Make the runs consistent (for example 2-2-2-2-2 or 3-3-3) so the stair-step is regular

Regular steps read as a clean line. Irregular ones look like mistakes. Also remove corner doubles.

3. Quick check back ↑

B. Draw a flat one-colour silhouette and check it reads at real size

If the silhouette is not recognisable at small size, polishing will not save it. Players recognise characters by silhouette in motion.

4. Check 1 back ↑

C. Mixed pixel sizes (mixels): the two sprites have different on-screen pixel grids

Pick one base resolution and scale; every sprite in a scene must share the same on-screen pixel grid size.

5. Check 2 back ↑

A. Pick one light direction and shade surfaces by how they face it instead of following the outline

Outline-following pillow shading ignores the light source; shading must come from a fixed light direction.
