Light, shading, dithering and outlines

Putting form into a sprite: a light source, banding, dithering and anti-aliasing by hand, and outline styles.

Pick one light source

Choose a direction (commonly top-left, sometimes top-right) and keep it for every object in the game. Then shade by form:

  • Surfaces facing the light get the lightest tone, surfaces turning away get the base tone, and surfaces facing away get the shadow tone.
  • A sphere has a highlight near the light side, a mid area, a core shadow a bit in from the edge on the dark side, and often a faint reflected light right at the edge. That tiny lighter rim on the dark side separates the object from the background.
  • A cube is easier: top face lightest, lit side mid, shadow side darkest, with flat shapes per face.
  • Cast shadows ground an object: a simple dark ellipse under a character helps it sit in the world.

Banding

Banding is when shading steps form lines parallel to the outline or evenly spaced stripes. It looks like contour lines on a map. Break it up: vary the width of the bands, let a lighter region wrap around the form instead of repeating the outline shape, and use fewer, bolder shapes.

Dithering

Dithering mixes two colours in a pattern so the eye sees something in between. With a small palette it creates gradients and texture.

  • Checkerboard (50% blend) and ordered patterns (Bayer matrices: 25%, 50%, 75% fills) look regular and “retro”.
  • Noise dithering looks organic but dirty at low resolution.
  • At game resolution it can shimmer when moving. Use it on static backgrounds and large surfaces, and sparingly on sprites. Hand-placed dither at the boundary between two ramp steps is more elegant than pattern fills everywhere.

Anti-aliasing by hand

Adding an intermediate colour in the corner of a stair-step smooths a curve. It is useful on big, static, high-contrast shapes (a moon, a logo). On sprites that move over changing backgrounds, AA pixels that match one background look like halos on another, so keep sprite edges hard or use outlines.

Outlines

  • Black or very dark outline: strongest separation from any background; classic look.
  • Coloured outline (a dark shade of the fill): softer, more painterly, still readable.
  • Selective outline (“selout”): the outline gets lighter where the form catches light (outline colour follows the adjacent fill), so the sprite feels lit all the way to the edge.
  • No outline for background elements keeps attention on the foreground. Make sure foreground sprites are separated by value then.
  • Inner lines: only where they describe a form change; too many lines flatten the shading.

Simulation: Banding and dithering

A smooth horizontal gradient squeezed into a few colours. Compare the patterns.

Try this in the full app:

  • With none and 3 colours you see hard bands. The eye notices the edges between them.
  • Choose ordered 4×4: the same 3 colours now suggest many more tones.
  • Compare 2×2 (coarse, few extra tones) with 8×8 (finer, closer to a true gradient).
  • Try random noise: smooth in tone but grainy. Which would you use on a tiny sprite?
  • Raise the colours to 8: at some point you need much less dithering.

Shading must come from a light source. Make one side lit and one shaded, with shapes that describe the form.

Patterns that move across the pixel grid shimmer and add noise that distracts from the sprite's shape.

It keeps the separation of an outline while letting the lit side feel lit.
