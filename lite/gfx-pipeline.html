From sprite to screen

Coordinate spaces, draw order, draw calls and batching, blending, filtering and the costs you can actually control.

The journey of one sprite

A character sprite is a rectangle of pixels in a texture. To appear on screen it passes through these steps, in every engine, whether you see them or not:

  1. Placement. The sprite has a position, rotation and scale in its parent's space, and parents have parents: its world transform is the product of all of them (see the transforms lesson).
  2. Camera. The world is transformed into view space: moved and scaled so the camera is at the origin. Zoom is a scale, shake is an offset.
  3. Culling. Sprites completely outside the view are skipped.
  4. Sorting. What is drawn on top of what.
  5. Submission. The CPU packs the vertices and the texture reference into draw calls for the GPU.
  6. GPU. Each sprite becomes two triangles. For every screen pixel the triangles cover, a fragment shader samples the texture and outputs a colour, which is blended with what is already there.
  7. Presentation. The finished frame is shown (VSync decides when).

Most "the click is in the wrong place" and "the HUD moves with the camera" bugs are a mix-up of spaces. Name your variables by space (world_pos, screen_pos) and convert explicitly (Godot: get_global_mouse_position(), canvas_transform).

Draw order

2D has no depth buffer by default: later is on top. Order is decided by (1) layers (background, world, effects, UI), (2) a z-index, and (3) the order in the scene tree. For top-down and isometric games, y-sorting draws things lower on the screen later, so a character standing behind a tree is hidden and in front of it is not: use the sprite's feet (its pivot at the base) as the sort key, and make the pivot consistent across all sprites. In Godot enable y_sort_enabled on the parent and give sprites an offset so their origin is at the feet.

Draw calls and batching

Each time the CPU tells the GPU "draw this with that texture and that state" costs a roughly fixed overhead, independent of how many pixels it draws. A thousand small sprites in a thousand calls is far slower than the same thousand in a few calls. Batching merges sprites that share the same texture, material and blend mode into one call. How to help it:

  • Use a texture atlas (many sprites packed into one image) so different sprites share one texture. Pack related things together (all enemies of a level).
  • Avoid breaking batches: switching texture, shader, blend mode or clip rectangle between sprites forces a new call. Sort by material when order does not matter.
  • Prefer tile maps and multi-mesh instancing for thousands of identical things.
  • Check your engine's monitors for draw calls (Godot: Debugger → Monitors → Draw calls) and see how it changes as you add things.

Blending

  • Alpha blend (normal): result = src * a + dst * (1 - a). Needs correct draw order.
  • Additive: dst + src. Glows, fire, magic. Order does not matter, and it can only brighten.
  • Multiply: darkens, for shadows and tinting.
  • Premultiplied alpha: the colour is already multiplied by alpha in the texture. Filtering and scaling look correct (no dark halos around sprite edges), and it is what engines often use internally. Mixing straight and premultiplied textures is the usual cause of white or black fringes.

Texture filtering

Linear filtering blends neighbouring texels and makes scaled art smooth, which is wrong for pixel art; use nearest. Mipmaps store smaller pre-filtered versions for textures shown small (3D, zooming out) and prevent shimmering; turn them off for pixel art. A texel is a pixel of the texture; the sampling point rarely lands exactly on one, which is why scaling by non-integers and moving by sub-pixels needs the care described in the pixel art track.

Resolution and scaling

Decide a base resolution and a stretch policy: pixel art scales by integers with letterboxing (the integer scaling drill); high-definition games usually keep the world's visible height fixed and let the width vary with the aspect ratio, anchoring the UI to the edges. Test extreme aspect ratios (ultrawide, a phone in portrait) and safe areas (notches).

Overdraw and fill rate

Transparent pixels cost as much as opaque ones: a full-screen translucent layer, or fifty stacked soft particles, makes the GPU shade the same pixels many times. Mobile and integrated GPUs run out of fill rate long before they run out of triangles. Trim transparent borders of sprites, avoid large soft sprites, and use opaque backgrounds where possible.

Performance checklist

  1. Measure draw calls and frame time before changing anything.
  2. Atlases and shared materials.
  3. Cull what is off-screen (engines do this for you, but your own effects might not).
  4. Watch overdraw (a debug overdraw view exists in most engines).
  5. Don't change shader parameters per sprite if you can avoid it: it breaks batches (use instance parameters or vertex colour).
